# Round 6 — Behavioral (STAR)
**Interview:** Competitive-Programming-Heavy / Quant-Adjacent Company

---

## Q1 — Conflict / Contest Prep
> "Tell me about a disagreement with a study partner or teammate during contest prep (like ICPC prep) on which approach to a problem was right."

*What I'm listening for:* Can you separate your ego from your code? In CP, the judge is absolute. If you argue an O(N^2) solution is "good enough" when N=10^5, you are factually wrong. How do you handle being proven wrong?

---

## Q2 — Failure / Contest Time Management
> "Tell me about a contest where your time management across the whole set failed you. Not one problem you scrapped, but a case where you misjudged the overall pacing and it cost you rank."

*Expected:* "I spent 45 minutes on Q3 trying to force a DP solution when it was actually a greedy problem, leaving me only 10 minutes for Q4 which was a standard graph problem I could have solved in 20. I learned to set a hard 20-minute cap before moving on and returning later."

---

## Q3 — Pressure / Handling a Rating Drop
> "How do you handle a rating drop after a bad contest? Walk me through what that actually feels like and what you changed afterward."

*Expected:* Honesty. It hurts. But what did you do? Did you immediately try to solve problems above your level to "win it back" (tilt), or did you upsolve the specific contest to find the gap in your knowledge?

---

## Q4 — Ambiguity / Self-Directed Study
> "With 1,500+ problems and no fixed curriculum, how did you actually decide what to study next at any given point?"

*Expected:* Did you just grind random problems (inefficient), or did you identify weaknesses (e.g., "I realized I was failing every segment tree problem, so I spent a week doing only range-query problems")? 

---

## Q5 — Initiative / Beyond the Coursework
> "What's something CP-related you taught yourself that wasn't covered in any DTU coursework?"

*Expected:* DTU covers basic graphs and DP. Fenwick trees, Segment Trees with lazy propagation, Trie-based bitwise operations, or heavy-light decomposition are usually self-taught. Talk about the moment you realized the standard curriculum wasn't enough to compete.
